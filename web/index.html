    Why two numbers and not one skill score

    A single score has to collapse both directions into one figure, and the weighting it picks is rarely the one your decision uses. The Heidke Skill Score, for instance, is rewarded by correct “no rain” calls — so it rates a mostly-dry season as the better-forecast one at the very moment a farmer is seeing more false alarms, not fewer. Reporting the two directions separately avoids choosing a weighting on the reader's behalf: whichever way your forecast is pointing today, the relevant number is right there. The seasonal split shows that inversion in full.

    Read the trend from the paired test, not the overlapping bands

    These numbers are measured against rain-gauge truth. Scored against the ERA5 reanalysis instead — a common shortcut — the same forecast looks roughly twice as good. That trap is its own section below.

                Why the confidence intervals are wider than you might expect

                Rainfall is correlated across the subcontinent

                Correlation between the wet/dry series of two locations, against the distance between them. It never reaches zero — the monsoon keeps the whole country partly in step.

                Pooling locations with different climatologies inflates the skill score

                Pooled against stratified, by metric and lead

                “Pooled” sums all 139 contingency tables and then scores. “Stratified” scores each location against its own table and averages the scores — the standard remedy. The gap is the size of the Hamill–Juras effect, with a paired 95% interval from the same 8° block bootstrap. The last three columns are the spread of the per-location scores, which is what the pooled figure hides.

                Known gaps

                  Limitations

                  • One model. ECMWF IFS025, not the IMD district advisories that farmers actually receive, and not “forecasts” in general.
                  • Grid cells, not districts. IMD’s district tables are not retrievable for past dates, so this verifies at grid points. Any district figure derived here would be an area-average approximation.
                  • Thresholds are conventional, not agronomic. 1 mm and 2.5 mm are standard wet-day definitions; neither is tied to a real spray decision.
                  • Only 12 spatial blocks. 8° over India yields 12 very unequal blocks, so the interval endpoints are themselves noisy. Do not read them to three decimals.
                  • 31 months, two monsoons. Not a climatology.
                  • Skill, not calibration.
