      Why false alarm ratio, and not a standard skill score

      A false alarm is a spray the farmer skipped and should have made — the error with a direct cost attached. Conventional skill scores disagree with it, and not by a little: the Heidke Skill Score rates the dry season as the better-forecast one while the false alarm ratio rates it worse, because HSS is rewarded by the abundant correct “no rain” calls of a season that is mostly dry. The seasonal split shows the inversion.

      Read the trend from the paired test, not the overlapping bands

      These numbers are measured against rain-gauge truth. Scored against the ERA5 reanalysis instead — a common shortcut — the same forecast looks roughly twice as good. That trap is its own section below.

      Yes — at every lead from one to seven days, but only when protecting costs between about 6% and 56% of the loss

      These are national figures, pooled over all 139 cells. The tool above is per-cell, so its break-even window will differ from this one.

      Value of acting on the forecast, versus the best you could do from climatology alone

      The cost–loss ratio α is what protective action costs divided by the loss it avoids. Value 1 would be perfect foresight; 0 means no better than the simple climatological rule. Above zero, the forecast is worth acting on.

                    Limitations

                    • One model. ECMWF IFS025, not the IMD district advisories that farmers actually receive, and not “forecasts” in general.
                    • Grid cells, not districts. IMD’s district tables are not retrievable for past dates, so this verifies at grid points. Any district figure derived here would be an area-average approximation.
                    • Thresholds are conventional, not agronomic. 1 mm and 2.5 mm are standard wet-day definitions; neither is tied to a real spray decision.
                    • Only 12 spatial blocks. 8° over India yields 12 very unequal blocks, so the interval endpoints are themselves noisy. Do not read them to three decimals.
                    • 31 months, two monsoons. Not a climatology.
                    • Skill, not calibration.

                    What is new here, and what is not

                      The place lookup is not analysis data

                      The decision tool turns a typed place name into coordinates so it can snap to the nearest of the 139 grid cells. That lookup is 700 Indian places — every state and district headquarters plus the most populous remaining towns — from GeoNames, CC BY 4.0, rebuilt by src/build_places.py. No number on this page is derived from it. It only decides which measured cell you are shown; the measurement itself is unchanged whether you reach a cell by typing, by map click, or by browser location.
